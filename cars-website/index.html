An argument in three cars

The three greatest cars in the world.

Thousands of cars have been fast. A handful have been beautiful. Only a very few changed what people believed a car could be. These three did — one on the racetrack, one on the spec sheet, and one by simply refusing to go away for sixty years.

Maranello, Italy

Ferrari 250 GTO

1962 – 1964 · 36 built · 3.0-litre Colombo V12

Ferrari built thirty-six of them, and then the world spent sixty years deciding they were the finest cars ever made. The 250 GTO was never intended as a monument. It was a homologation special — a racing car built in just enough numbers to be allowed to race, wearing a body that Ferrari's aerodynamicists had shaped by trial, error and instinct rather than by any wind tunnel.

What emerged was a car with no wasted line. The nose is low and predatory, the headlights buried under perspex, the tail chopped flat in the then-radical Kamm style because the airflow did not care about beauty and got it anyway. Behind the front wheels sit three small louvres, cut by hand, positioned by eye. Every one of the thirty-six cars is subtly different from the others.

Underneath is the reason it matters: the 3.0-litre Colombo V12, six twin-choke Weber carburettors, around 300 horsepower in a car weighing barely 880 kilograms. It won the International Championship for GT Manufacturers three years running, from 1962 to 1964, and it was quick at Le Mans, at the Targa Florio, and at the Tour de France Automobile.

It is now the most valuable car on earth. A 250 GTO reportedly changed hands privately for around $70 million, and the public auction record — set in 2018 — stands above $48 million. But price is the least interesting thing about it. The 250 GTO is the moment when racing engineering and sculpture stopped being separate disciplines.

Woking, England

McLaren F1

1992 – 1998 · 106 built · 6.1-litre BMW V12

Gordon Murray had just spent a career designing Formula One cars when he sat down in an airport lounge in Milan and sketched a road car with three seats — the driver in the middle, a passenger set back on each side. Nobody had asked for it. Nobody has really done it since. That sketch became the McLaren F1, and it is the closest the car industry has come to an engineer being given everything he wanted and no reason to compromise.

The rules Murray set were absolute. No turbochargers, because he wanted throttle response. No traction control, no ABS, no power steering, because they added weight and filtered the car. A carbon-fibre monocoque — the first ever on a production road car. And when BMW Motorsport delivered a naturally aspirated 6.1-litre V12 making 618 horsepower, Murray lined the engine bay in gold foil, because gold is the best reflector of heat there is.

The result weighed 1 138 kilograms. In 1998 an F1 with its rev limiter raised was driven to 240.1 mph at Volkswagen's Ehra-Lessien proving ground, a record for a production car that stood for years — and remains the record for a naturally aspirated one. Two years earlier, a barely modified racing version had won the 24 Hours of Le Mans outright, first time out, against purpose-built prototypes.

Only 106 were built, of which 64 were road cars. Murray never intended the F1 to race at all. It won Le Mans anyway. That is the whole story of the car in one sentence.

Stuttgart-Zuffenhausen, Germany

Porsche 911

1963 – today · 1 000 000+ built · rear-mounted flat-six

Every engineering instinct says the Porsche 911 should not work. The engine hangs behind the rear axle, which is the wrong place for it — a pendulum bolted to the back of the car, forever trying to overtake the front. In 1963 that was an economy-car layout carried over from the Volkswagen Beetle. Six decades later Porsche is still building it, and it is still the sharpest thing in its class.

Nobody at Porsche defended the layout on paper. They simply refused to abandon it, and then spent sixty years engineering around the problem: longer wheelbases, wider rear track, the Weissach rear axle, then all-wheel drive, then rear-wheel steering. What began as a flaw became the 911's signature — extraordinary traction out of slow corners, because all the weight sits exactly where the driven wheels are.

The first car had a 2.0-litre flat-six making 130 hp. The current 911 GT3 runs a naturally aspirated 4.0-litre revving to 9 000 rpm. In between it won the Monte Carlo Rally, the Targa Florio, the Daytona 24 Hours, and class victory at Le Mans more times than any other model line. It is also, uniquely among great cars, the one you can genuinely use every day — which is why more than a million have been sold.

The 250 GTO is the most beautiful car ever built and the McLaren F1 is the most uncompromising. But the 911 is the greatest achievement of the three, because greatness that lasts sixty years and eight generations is much harder than greatness that lasts one.
